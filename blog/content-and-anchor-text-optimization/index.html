Optimization of Content and Anchor Text: What Actually Matters

8 November 2026 · 3 min read

Take a plumber in Leeds called Dave. His website has a page about boiler repairs, and he links to it from the homepage with the words "click here". Fine, technically. But that link says nothing, and Google has nothing to go on. Fixing it takes him thirty seconds. That's the heart of anchor text, and it's why content and links need tuning together.

So, what is anchor text?

It's the clickable part of a link. When Dave swaps "click here" for "boiler repair in Leeds", that phrase becomes the anchor. Google reads those words as a hint about what the other page covers. Readers treat them like a small promise: click this, get that.

The flavours

Anchors come in a few flavours, and a healthy mix looks natural. Exact match uses the keyword as it is, like "boiler repair". Partial match tucks it inside something longer, such as "how we handle boiler repair jobs". Branded anchors use a name, like "Dave's Plumbing". Generic ones ("read more") say very little. Naked URLs just print the address, which is fine for sources and clumsy anywhere else.

Where people slip up

Dave's first instinct was to write "boiler repair Leeds" on every single link, all over the site. It looks forced, and readers notice. The other trap is linking a random word because you need a link somewhere. A link belongs in a sentence that's really about its target. On our own site, a post about headlines would point to the Title Case Maker, and one about page titles would send people to SEO Title Length.

A quick before and after

Before: "For more tools, click here." After: "Browse the free text tools for cleaning up a messy paste." Same page, same link. The second tells readers what's coming and tells Google what the page is about. Honestly, that's most of the trick. One more pair: swap "this article" for "our guide to cleaning up copied text". Longer, yes, but the link now explains itself even to someone skimming with half-closed eyes.

How many links is too many?

No magic number here. A short post with twelve links to the same two pages looks desperate. A long guide with three links to unrelated pages feels thin. My test is simple: if a link makes the reader's next click obvious, keep it. If it only exists because you wanted a link there, drop it.

Fix the page, not just the link

Even a perfect anchor can't rescue a weak page. Go for one topic, a quick opening and zero padding. Check your length with Word Count, and if the draft feels bloated, Reduce Word Count will flag the filler. Sort out your main phrase and a few variations in the Keyword Phrase Maker, then make sure the title still fits.

Test every link

Dave redesigned his site last spring and half his links broke. He found out in autumn, from a customer. Don't be Dave. Paste your URLs into the Bulk Link Opener and each one pops open in its own tab, so a dead page jumps out. Do it after any redesign, because that's exactly when links snap.

A routine worth repeating

Here it is in a nutshell. Pick one main keyword per page and write for the reader first. Trim the filler, then confirm the length and the title. Add two or three internal links with different anchors, open each one to be sure it works, and revisit older posts every few months to refresh the weak anchors.
